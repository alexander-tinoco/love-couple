What I changed after looking at the photos

  
The reference shots corrected two things I had guessed wrong, and both were making it look like a toy.


  

    

      
1. There is a pupil, and it is a separate shape

      
Every photo shows the same structure: a large coloured body with a second, distinctly different shape inside it — a round pupil, a tall slit, even a lightning bolt. I had been drawing one solid blob. The pupil is what gives it a direction of gaze and, more than anything, what makes size mean something: a tiny pupil in a wide eye reads instantly as shock.

    

    

      
2. The fills are flat, not gradients

      
I had assumed a gradient. Looking closely at the gold and orange shots, the colours are completely flat — the soft shading you see is the physical dome glass over the display, not the pixels. So gradient now defaults to almost nothing, and the depth comes from the contrast between body and pupil instead. Cheaper to draw and closer to the original.

    

    

      
3. Upright ovals, not tilted rectangles

      
The eyes are big, near-elliptical and vertical — barely tilted at all in the neutral state. Tilt is expression, not the resting pose, so it now defaults to zero and only a few moods use it.

    

    

      
4. Particles are part of the vocabulary

      
One shot has small red hearts drifting up across the whole screen, over the eyes. That layer is cheap to draw and does a lot of emotional work. In love uses it, with heart-shaped pupils to match.

    

    

      
5. Eyelids must be clipped to their own eye

      
A tilted eyelid is a rotated rectangle, and a rotated rectangle wide enough to cover one eye is wide enough to swallow the other. Half the moods were painting a black wedge across the whole display. Each lid is now clipped to a box around its own eye — and to a rectangle, not to the eye outline, because erasing through an antialiased curved clip leaves a ghost outline of the fully open eye floating in the dark.

    

    

      
6. The glow belongs to the final silhouette

      
Glowing each eye before drawing its lid leaks a halo out from under every closed eyelid — a bright pink rim around a shape that is supposed to be shut. The eyes are now drawn to an offscreen buffer, lids erased rather than painted black, and the whole result is blurred and composited underneath itself. On the ESP32 the same rule holds: build the visible shape first, glow second.

    

  


  

    And a real bug you caught
    
You were right that Angry looked sad. The eyelid was rotated by side · brow, which tips the outer corners down instead of the inner ones — the exact inversion of an angry brow. It is now −side · brow, and I re-checked all sixteen moods against it. The full audit is in the table below.
